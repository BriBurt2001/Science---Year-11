🧬 DNA, Genes and Making Proteins

1. What is DNA?

DNA is the molecule that stores genetic information. Its full name is deoxyribonucleic acid. The "deoxyribo" part names its sugar. The "nucleic acid" part tells us it was first found in the nucleus and is slightly acidic.

In plants, animals and fungi, almost all DNA sits inside the nucleus of every cell. Small amounts also occur in mitochondria and chloroplasts. Bacteria have no nucleus, so their DNA lies loose in the cytoplasm.

DNA contains all the instructions a cell needs to function, grow and reproduce. In this sense, DNA works like a recipe book. The cell reads only the recipes it needs at a given time.

Big idea: DNA is an information store. The information is held in the order of its bases, much as meaning in a sentence is held in the order of its letters.

2. The structure of DNA

DNA is a long polymer. A polymer is a large molecule built from many repeating units. In DNA, these units are called nucleotides.

Each nucleotide has three parts:

  • a phosphate group (the small circles in the diagram);
  • a deoxyribose sugar (the pentagons);
  • a nitrogenous base (the coloured shapes).

The sugar of one nucleotide bonds to the phosphate of the next. This forms a strong sugar–phosphate backbone. Two backbones sit side by side, like the rails of a ladder. The paired bases form the rungs.

Finally, the ladder twists. This gives DNA its famous shape, the double helix. "Double" refers to the two strands. "Helix" means a spiral, like a spring or a spiral staircase.

3. The four bases and the base pair rule

DNA uses four nitrogenous bases:

A Adenine   T Thymine   G Guanine   C Cytosine

Bases on opposite strands always pair in the same way. This is the base pair rule, also called complementary base pairing:

A pairs with T.   G pairs with C.
Memory hook: "Apples in Trees, Grapes in Cars."

Weak hydrogen bonds hold each pair together. They are weak enough to "unzip" when the cell needs to read or copy DNA. Yet millions of them together keep the molecule stable.

The rule has a useful consequence. If you know one strand, you can predict the other. For example, the strand A T G C C A pairs with T A C G G T. It also means the amount of A always equals T, and G always equals C, in double‑stranded DNA. This pattern is known as Chargaff's rule.

4. What is a gene?

A gene is a section of DNA that codes for a protein. One DNA molecule can hold hundreds or thousands of genes, spaced along its length.

DNA is packaged in a tidy way. Each very long DNA molecule coils tightly around proteins to form a chromosome. Humans have 46 chromosomes in most body cells. All of the DNA in an organism is its genome.

Nested sizes, largest to smallest: organism → cell → nucleus → chromosome → DNA molecule → gene → base pair.

5. Proteins and alleles

A protein is a long chain of smaller units called amino acids. There are 20 kinds of amino acid. Their order makes each protein unique.

The logic runs in a chain. The sequence of bases in a gene sets the sequence of amino acids. The sequence of amino acids sets how the chain folds, which gives the protein its shape. A protein's shape decides its function. So, a change in the bases can change a protein's job.

Proteins do many jobs in cells and bodies. For example:

  • Haemoglobin carries oxygen inside red blood cells.
  • Enzymes, such as amylase, speed up chemical reactions.
  • Keratin and collagen give structure to hair, skin and tendons.
  • Proteins involved in making melanin give colour to skin, hair and eyes.

[Accuracy note: the worksheet calls melanin a protein. Melanin is in fact a pigment. Enzymes, which are proteins, such as tyrosinase, build it. Genes therefore still control skin colour, but they do so through these proteins.]

Alleles

Different versions of the same gene are called alleles. Alleles differ slightly in their sequence of A, T, C and G. These small differences can change the protein made. This is one reason people differ in traits such as eye colour or blood group.

In te ao Māori, genetic material connects to whakapapa (genealogy) and may be seen as taonga (a treasure). For this reason, consent and respect matter whenever DNA is collected, stored or studied.

6. Making proteins: from gene to protein

DNA stays safely in the nucleus. Proteins, however, are built on ribosomes in the cytoplasm. The cell solves this problem with a two‑step process called protein synthesis.

Step 1: Transcription

In the nucleus, the DNA of one gene unzips. A copy of the gene is made as messenger RNA (mRNA), using the base pair rule. In RNA, uracil (U) replaces thymine, so A pairs with U. The mRNA then leaves the nucleus.

Step 2: Translation

At a ribosome, the mRNA is read three bases at a time. Each group of three is a codon. Each codon matches one amino acid. Transfer RNA (tRNA) brings the right amino acid. The ribosome links them into a chain.

Step 3: Folding

The finished chain folds into its 3D shape. The shape lets the protein do its function. A stop codon, such as UAA, ends the chain.

Flow of information: DNA → (transcription) → mRNA → (translation) → protein → trait.
Maths link: one codon = 3 bases = 1 amino acid. So 300 coding bases make 100 amino acids.

A worked example

DNA template strand: TAC AAA CCG ATT
mRNA: AUG UUU GGC UAA
Protein: Met – Phe – Gly – stop

8. Key terms

TermMeaning
NucleotideThe monomer of DNA: phosphate + deoxyribose sugar + base.
Double helixTwo DNA strands twisted into a spiral.
Complementary base pairingA with T (or U in RNA); G with C.
Hydrogen bondA weak bond that holds base pairs together.
GeneA section of DNA that codes for one protein.
AlleleA different version of the same gene.
ChromosomeA long DNA molecule coiled tightly around proteins.
GenomeAll the genetic material of an organism.
mRNAA single‑stranded copy of a gene that carries the code to a ribosome.
CodonThree mRNA bases that code for one amino acid (or stop).
RibosomeThe organelle where amino acids are joined into a protein.
Amino acidThe monomer of a protein. There are 20 types.
